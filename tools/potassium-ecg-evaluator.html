Hypokalemia & Ventricular Arhythmia

When serum potassium falls below 3.5 mEq/L, myocardial cell membranes become hyperpolarized, delaying Phase 3 repolarization. This prolongation generates prominent U waves and ST-segment depression. As the T wave fuses with the U wave, the resulting prolonged QU interval creates high susceptibility to dangerous re-entrant ventricular arrhythmias, including Torsades de Pointes.

Hyperkalemia & The Classical "Sine Wave"

Elevated potassium (> 5.5 mEq/L) accelerates repolarization, producing early tall, peaked T waves. As concentrations rise above 7.0 - 7.9 mEq/L, cardiac sodium channel inactivation slows impulse conduction across the ventricles. The P wave flattens and disappears, while the QRS complex widens significantly, merging into the T wave to form a lethal Sine Wave pattern preceding cardiac arrest.

Scientific Framework & Clinical Guidelines

The electrophysiological relationships mapped in this diagnostic tool correspond with international cardiology consensus standards and local Singapore clinical practice guidelines:

  • European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines on Acute Electrocardiography: Definitive diagnostic criteria linking serum potassium alterations to membrane resting potentials and cardiac action potential Phase 0–4 kinetics.
  • MOH ACE Clinical Guidance on Electrolyte & Renal Management: Ministry of Health Singapore guidance outlining safety protocols, medication-induced potassium imbalances (ACEi/ARB/MRA therapies), and emergency monitoring thresholds.
  • Singapore Renal Registry (SRR) Data: Clinical epidemiologic tracking establishing chronic kidney disease (CKD) as the primary catalyst for chronic hyperkalemia in local patient populations.
  • SingHealth & National Heart Centre Singapore (NHCS) Clinical Protocols: Institutional protocols defining immediate IV calcium stabilization requirements upon detection of hyperkalemic QRS widening or sinusoidal ECG configurations.
