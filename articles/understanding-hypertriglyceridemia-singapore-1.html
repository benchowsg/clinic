The Forgotten Blood Fat: Why High Triglycerides Silently Strain Your Heart & Liver

Triglycerides are not caused by fatty foods alone. Discover how hidden sugars, refined hawker carbs, and insulin resistance drive hypertriglyceridemia in Singapore.

Written & Reviewed by Dr. Gerald Thang

Principal Physician

MBBS (Singapore), MRCP (UK), Graduate Diploma in Cardiology (Singapore)

⚡ Quick Summary: Hypertriglyceridemia Insights

  • Sugar to Fat Conversion: Triglycerides are unused energy stored in blood. The liver rapidly converts excess refined carbohydrates, fructose, and alcohol into triglycerides.
  • The TG-to-HDL Ratio: A high Triglyceride-to-HDL ratio indicates insulin resistance and small, dense LDL particles that easily embed into arterial walls.
  • Fatty Liver & Pancreatitis Risk: High triglycerides drive fat accumulation in liver cells (MASLD) and, at severe levels (> 5.6 mmol/L), can trigger acute pancreatitis.
  • Reversible Condition: Cutting liquid sugars, substituting refined carbs with whole grains, and increasing aerobic physical activity can rapidly reduce triglyceride levels.

When patients review their lipid panel after a routine health screening in Singapore, their attention almost always jumps straight to LDL Cholesterol ("bad cholesterol"). However, there is another critical blood lipid that is frequently overlooked: Triglycerides.

While cholesterol is used by the body to build cell membranes and hormones, triglycerides represent unused calories stored as fat in the bloodstream. Having high triglycerides—a condition known medically as Hypertriglyceridemia—is a major hallmark of underlying insulin resistance, fatty liver disease, and heightened cardiovascular risk.

How Triglycerides Leave the "Optimal Zone": Triggers & Aggravating Factors

Triglycerides do not become elevated overnight. Because they represent unused energy circulating in your blood, your levels rise whenever your body receives more simple energy than its metabolic engine can burn or clear efficiently.

The primary triggers that push triglycerides out of the optimal zone include:

  • High-Glycemic & Refined Carbohydrate Intake: Simple sugars, sweetened teas, fruit juices, and refined starches enter the bloodstream rapidly. When glycogen stores are full, the liver converts excess glucose into triglycerides through de novo lipogenesis.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Even moderate alcohol intake boosts triglyceride production. Alcohol suppresses fatty acid breakdown in the liver, forcing unused fats to be bundled into triglycerides and dumped back into circulation.
  • Insulin Resistance & Sedentary Habits: When muscle cells become resistant to insulin, they stop absorbing glucose effectively. The liver steps in to convert this surplus sugar straight into triglycerides. Lack of exercise also reduces lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity, decreasing fat clearance.
  • Visceral Fat & Weight Gain: Fat stored around internal organs actively releases free fatty acids directly into the liver via the portal vein, driving continuous triglyceride synthesis around the clock.
  • Secondary Medical Factors: Conditions such as unmanaged thyroid imbalances or kidney strain, as well as specific long-term medications, can subtly impair fat clearance mechanisms.

🔄 The Vicious Cycle of High Triglycerides

As triglycerides remain elevated, they further impair insulin sensitivity in the liver. This creates a compounding loop: higher triglycerides lead to greater insulin resistance, which in turn causes the liver to produce even more triglycerides.

Triglyceride Ranges & Clinical Risk Levels

Triglyceride Band Level (mmol/L) Primary Clinical Risk
Normal < 1.7 mmol/L Low baseline cardiovascular and metabolic risk.
Borderline High 1.7 – 2.2 mmol/L Early indicator of insulin resistance and dietary sugar surplus.
High 2.3 – 5.6 mmol/L Accelerated arterial plaque risk; strongly associated with Fatty Liver (MASLD).
Very High (Severe) > 5.6 mmol/L High acute risk of Acute Pancreatitis (severe pancreatic inflammation).

4 Practical Steps to Lower Triglycerides Naturally

The encouraging news about hypertriglyceridemia is that it is highly responsive to targeted lifestyle modifications. Because triglycerides reflect recent calorie and sugar processing, levels can improve rapidly with the right changes:

  • Eliminate Liquid Sugars: Switch sweetened coffees, teas, and soft drinks to "Kosong" (zero sugar) options. Fructose in liquid sugar bypasses normal liver metabolic checks and turns directly into triglycerides.
  • Swap Refined Carbs for Whole Grains: Reduce white rice and refined flour portions ("Shao Fan") in favor of unrefined complex carbohydrates and high-fiber vegetables.
  • Incorporate Regular Aerobic Exercise: Brisk walking, cycling, or swimming activates Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL), an enzyme that clears triglycerides from your bloodstream to fuel working muscles.
  • Boost Omega-3 Intake: Consume oily fish (saba, salmon) or high-grade omega-3 EPA/DHA, which slows down liver triglyceride synthesis.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is hypertriglyceridemia and how does it differ from cholesterol?

Triglycerides are unused calories stored as fat in your blood for energy, whereas cholesterol is a waxy lipid used to build cells and hormones. Elevated triglycerides stem largely from excess refined carbohydrates, sugars, and alcohol.

Why is a high Triglyceride-to-HDL ratio dangerous for the heart?

A high Triglyceride-to-HDL ratio is a key marker of insulin resistance and small, dense LDL particles. These small LDL particles easily penetrate blood vessel walls, accelerating arterial plaque formation and heart disease risk.

Can high triglycerides cause pancreatitis or fatty liver disease?

Yes. Very high triglyceride levels (above 5.6 mmol/L) can trigger acute pancreatitis, a severe abdominal inflammation. Chronically high triglycerides also drive fat accumulation in the liver, leading to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).
